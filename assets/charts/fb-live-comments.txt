FB LIVE COMMENTS — MOCK INTERVIEW SCRIPT
=========================================

The candidate knows the tech but has bad instincts about sequencing. Each scene
has one typical mistake, then the interviewer pulls them back.
Lines marked with > are director's notes, not spoken.


SCENE 0 — Opening. The candidate jumps straight to architecture.
----------------------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: Today we're designing comments for Facebook Live. People watching
a live video can post a comment underneath it, and they see other people's
comments too. You've got 45 minutes. Go ahead.

CANDIDATE: Okay, so I think we'll need a WebSocket connection, and then behind
that a Kafka topic, and once the comment is written we—

INTERVIEWER: Hold on. You're already picking technology. I haven't told you how
big this system is or who's using it.

CANDIDATE: Oh, right. Sorry. Let me back up and ask about requirements first.

> Don't apologize, just turn. But the turn itself is correct.

CANDIDATE: So a few things I want to nail down. First — "seeing other people's
comments," does that mean in real time, or is it fine if they show up on
refresh?

INTERVIEWER: Real time. Viewers should feel like they're in sync with the
broadcaster.

CANDIDATE: Okay, so a few hundred milliseconds. Is that the right ballpark?

INTERVIEWER: Yeah, let's say 200 milliseconds.

CANDIDATE: Second — if someone joins the stream halfway through, do they see
the comments from before they joined?

INTERVIEWER: Yes, they do.

CANDIDATE: And third, can you reply to a comment, or react to one?

INTERVIEWER: Not this round. Just the comments themselves.

> Those three questions each buy something. The first one decides push versus
> poll. The second one decides whether we need a database at all. The third one
> kills nesting and reaction counters, which makes the data model flat.


SCENE 1 — Locking down the functional requirements
---------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: Let me write down the functional requirements and you tell me if I
got them right.

  1. A viewer can post a comment under a live video
  2. A viewer sees new comments from other people in near real time (~200ms)
  3. A viewer can see comments posted before they joined

  Out of scope: replying to comments, reactions, reporting, deleting

INTERVIEWER: That works.

> Three sentences, twenty seconds. Every one of them is "what can a user do,"
> and none of them contain the words WebSocket, Kafka, or database. The out of
> scope line matters — it's you confirming the boundary so nobody says later
> that you missed something.


SCENE 2 — Non-functional requirements. The candidate is scared of numbers.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: Next I want to know the scale. How big is this?

INTERVIEWER: You tell me. It's Facebook Live.

CANDIDATE: Um... big.

INTERVIEWER: How big?

CANDIDATE: I don't know the real numbers, but let me reason about it. Live
streams happening at the same time, I'll say a million. Most of them have
almost nobody watching — it's a handful of celebrity streams and sports games
that actually matter. For a peak single stream, let's say two million
concurrent viewers, and maybe a few thousand comments per second. Is that the
right order of magnitude?

INTERVIEWER: That's reasonable.

> "I don't know" is fine, as long as you follow it with a number you can
> derive, and then ask the interviewer to confirm. Freezing loses points.
> Inventing a number with false confidence loses more.

CANDIDATE: Let me do one quick calculation before I go on. Two million viewers
on one stream. If every viewer receives every comment, that's 5,000 comments a
second times 2 million people — ten billion messages per second. Call it 200
bytes a comment, so, what, 2 terabytes per second of egress. That number can't
be real.

INTERVIEWER: So what?

CANDIDATE: So at the very top end, not everyone can receive everything. I'm
going to park that and come back to it when I get to that part of the design.

> This is the important bit in this scene. You found an absurd number — do NOT
> immediately start solving it. Park it and keep going. Junior candidates blow
> up here, start talking about sampling and CDNs, and run out of time before
> the requirements are even done.

CANDIDATE: One more thing on consistency. Is it okay if two viewers see
comments in a slightly different order?

INTERVIEWER: What do you think?

CANDIDATE: I think it's fine. Comments aren't money and they aren't inventory.
Nobody's going to notice the ordering. So I'd pick availability over
consistency, eventual consistency is fine.

There's one exception though. The person who wrote the comment — if they
refresh, they'd better see their own comment. That's read-after-write, and that
path can't be eventual.

INTERVIEWER: Good.

> That's the scoring moment. Not reciting CAP — saying "the system is AP, except
> this one path." If the interviewer pushes, add PACELC: even with no partition
> you're still choosing between latency and consistency, and the 200ms target
> means you picked latency.

CANDIDATE: So the non-functional requirements are:

  - A million concurrent streams; a few thousand comments/sec on a hot one
  - End to end under 200ms
  - Availability over consistency, eventual is fine
  - Except: the author reading their own comment needs read-after-write
  - A comment shouldn't be counted twice or shown twice

INTERVIEWER: Good, keep going.


SCENE 3 — Entities and API. The candidate wants to skip them.
--------------------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: Alright, so let me draw the architec—

INTERVIEWER: Give me the data first.

CANDIDATE: Okay. Three things:

  User        id, name, avatar
  LiveVideo   id, broadcaster_id, started_at   (another team owns this, I'm
                                                treating it as a black box)
  Comment     id, live_video_id, user_id, text, created_at

Comment is flat, because we ruled out replies and reactions earlier.

On the video side, I'm going to assume the streaming team hands me a stable
liveVideoId and tells me when a stream starts and ends. That's the entire
interface I need from them — I'm not designing encoding or segmenting. And if
their service is down, posting a comment just fails; I'm not going to try to
work around it on my side.

> "Another team owns this" is a good sentence. It closes off a whole area so the
> interviewer doesn't drag you into video streaming.
>
> Note the wording: "I'm going to assume," not "I expect they've done a good
> job." You're not vouching for another team's quality, you're stating an
> interface and a failure mode. If you only say the first half, the interviewer
> will ask "and what if that service is down?" — so say the second half yourself.
> ("assume" is the word here, by the way. "Presume" implies you have grounds to
> believe it; you're just setting a premise so you can move on.)

CANDIDATE: For the API:

  POST /comments/:liveVideoId          { text }
  GET  /comments/:liveVideoId?cursor={last_id}&limit=50

The second one is for "comments from before you joined." Cursor, not offset,
because comments keep arriving — with offset pagination you'd get duplicates or
skip things.

INTERVIEWER: What about the real-time one?

CANDIDATE: I haven't decided what that is yet. I'll fill it in once I get to
that part of the design.

> Much better than making up an endpoint on the spot. It also honestly flags
> where you are in the process.


SCENE 4 — The dumbest version that works
------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: Let me draw the simplest thing that satisfies all three functional
requirements.

  Client ──POST /comments──> Comment Service ──> DB
  Client ──GET  /comments──> Comment Service ──> DB

Comment gets written to the DB. To read comments you query the DB. For comments
from before you joined, you page backwards with the cursor. As for real time...
this version doesn't do real time. The client would just have to keep
re-requesting.

INTERVIEWER: How often would it re-request?

CANDIDATE: If we want it within 200 milliseconds, then every 200 milliseconds.
Two million viewers polling every 200ms is ten million requests a second, and
most of them come back empty. That doesn't work.

INTERVIEWER: So?

CANDIDATE: So the server has to push instead.

> Don't skip this scene. Draw the dumb version, then break it yourself. That's
> what earns every component you add later. People who draw the full
> architecture up front can't answer "why is that there."


SCENE 5 — Push. The candidate dumps three layers at once.
-----------------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: So I'll use WebSockets, and each server subscribes to a Kafka topic,
and then we shard with consistent hashing, and then—

INTERVIEWER: Slow down. Just the first decision. Why WebSockets?

CANDIDATE: Uh... because it's bidirectional.

INTERVIEWER: Does this problem need bidirectional?

CANDIDATE: Let me think... actually, not really. Way more people are watching
than commenting, and posting a comment is just a normal POST — I never use the
reverse channel. So let me switch to SSE, Server-Sent Events. It's one
direction, it rides on plain HTTP, and the browser reconnects on its own when
the connection drops.

INTERVIEWER: Good.

> This is the single best thing in the whole script: when you're asked for your
> reasoning and you realize you picked wrong, change your answer right there.
> Defending a bad choice costs more than switching. And notice the reason came
> from the requirements — "more people watch than comment" — not from memory.


SCENE 6 — Sharding, one layer at a time
-----------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: SSE is a long-lived connection, so a viewer is pinned to one
particular server. But the comment arrives on a different server — the one that
handled the POST. So I need something in between to broadcast. Simplest version
first:

  POST → Comment Service → DB
                         → Redis pub/sub (one channel, everything goes in)
                                ↓ every broadcast server subscribes
                           Broadcast Server → SSE → viewers

INTERVIEWER: What's wrong with that?

CANDIDATE: Every broadcast server receives comments for every live stream in the
world, but the viewers sitting on it only care about a few streams. With 500
servers, 99% of the message processing is wasted.

INTERVIEWER: How do you fix it?

CANDIDATE: Split it into channels. hash(liveVideoId) % N decides which channel a
stream's comments go to, and all the comments for one stream always land in the
same channel. A server only subscribes to the channels it needs.

The unit I'm sharding on is the stream, not the comment. You can't split a
single stream across channels — if you did, viewers on one server would only
get part of that stream's comments.

INTERVIEWER: Did that actually save you anything?

CANDIDATE: Let me think... no, not yet, actually. If every server has a few
viewers from every stream scattered across it, every server still has to
subscribe to all the channels.

INTERVIEWER: So what do you do?

CANDIDATE: I need viewers of the same stream to land on the same set of servers.
So the Dispatcher assigns streams to servers up front: "stream A is handled by
bc-07 and bc-08," and that assignment goes into etcd. bc-07 is watching etcd,
sees it's been given stream A, and subscribes to that stream's channel. Only
after that does the Dispatcher start sending stream A's viewers to bc-07.

The order matters — assign, then subscribe, then take viewers. If a server only
subscribed once its first viewer showed up, the comments in the gap before the
subscription takes effect are just gone, and that viewer has no way to know.

So now it does save something. Stream A's viewers are concentrated on two
servers, and those two only subscribe to A's channel instead of all five.

INTERVIEWER: How does the Dispatcher decide who gets what?

CANDIDATE: By current load and how hot the stream is, not by hashing. A stream
that just started gets one server. If the audience climbs, the Dispatcher pulls
in more servers and points new viewers at them, leaving existing connections
alone. That's what the lookup table buys you — hashing can't grow a stream's
capacity mid-flight.

And I wouldn't drop a subscription the moment the last viewer leaves. Say a
small stream has three viewers on bc-07 and all three close the tab — if bc-07
unsubscribes right away and two people join ten seconds later, it has to
subscribe again. Anyone who joins during the gap between unsubscribe and
resubscribe silently misses comments. So I'd wait five minutes after the last
viewer before dropping it. The cost of waiting is just receiving some comments
nobody wants, which is cheap.

> Watch the rhythm here. One layer at a time, and you wait for "does that
> actually work" before giving the next one. Dumping all three at once makes it
> impossible for the interviewer to tell whether you understand it or memorized
> it.


SCENE 7 — Disconnects
-----------------------

INTERVIEWER: What happens when the long-lived connection drops?

CANDIDATE: It will drop, and it'll drop constantly — phones switching networks,
elevators, backgrounding the app. Comments during that window are just missed.

SSE has an answer built in. Every event carries an id, and when the browser
reconnects it automatically sends Last-Event-ID in the header, so the server
knows where to resume.

INTERVIEWER: Resume how far back?

CANDIDATE: Not unlimited. I'd draw the line at five minutes. Past that, treat
them as a fresh join and just hit GET /comments for the latest batch. Somebody
who was gone for half an hour doesn't want to read half an hour of comments
anyway.

For those few minutes of replay I'd keep recent comments in Redis so I'm not
going back to the database for it.

INTERVIEWER: And when the phone backgrounds the app?

CANDIDATE: Close the connection deliberately before backgrounding, so the server
isn't holding an idle connection for nobody. Reconnect and catch up on
foreground. That's the frontend's job.


SCENE 8 — Back to that 2 TB/s
-------------------------------

CANDIDATE: I want to come back to the number I calculated earlier. At two
million viewers on one stream this whole SSE setup falls apart — a single server
can't hold that many connections, and pushing everything is still 2 TB/s.

INTERVIEWER: What do you do about it?

CANDIDATE: Two directions.

The first is sampling. Above some viewer count, the server decides to push only
a fraction of the comments to each viewer. Nobody can read 5,000 comments a
second anyway — what's scrolling past is really just a feeling of "this place is
busy."

The second one saves more. At that scale everyone can be looking at the same
thing. So every second, take the most recent 100 to 200 comments, write them as
a snapshot to the CDN, and have clients poll that instead of holding an SSE
connection. Two million viewers become one origin fetch as far as the backend is
concerned.

The cost is latency — 200 milliseconds becomes one to two seconds. I think
that's acceptable at that scale. On a stream with two million people, the
comments aren't a conversation anymore, they're atmosphere.

INTERVIEWER: When do you switch over?

CANDIDATE: Pick a threshold — say, above 500,000 concurrent viewers you flip to
CDN mode, and flip back when it drops. The actual number would come from
measuring it.

> You parked something, and now you unpark it at the right moment, with the cost
> and the switching condition. That lands better than opening with "we'll use a
> CDN," because now everyone listening knows which number it's solving.


SCENE 9 — Bringing up the frontend without being asked
--------------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: That's roughly the backend. I'd like to spend a few minutes on the
frontend, because there are a few places here where if the client gets it wrong
it doesn't matter how good the backend is.

INTERVIEWER: Go ahead.

CANDIDATE: First, the comments on screen actually come from two different
sources and you can't blur them together. The older comments came from an API
call — refetchable, cacheable. The ones coming in over SSE are a stream you
can't replay; if it breaks, it's broken. When you merge them into one list you
have to dedupe, because the comment you just posted comes back to you over SSE
as well. Match on the server-assigned id.

Second, posting a comment should be an optimistic update. Insert it locally in a
pending state, swap in the real id when the 201 comes back. On failure, mark it
red with a retry button — don't swallow it silently.

Third, the DOM needs a ceiling. Thousands of comments a second means the node
count grows without limit. I'd keep the last 500 and drop the oldest, plus a
virtualized list so only the visible ones actually render.

Fourth, there's a specific accessibility trap here. If the comment region uses
aria-live="assertive", every incoming comment interrupts whatever the screen
reader is in the middle of saying. On a stream with several comments a second
that makes it completely unusable by ear. It has to be "polite" so it finishes
the current sentence first. And the author name and the comment text need to be
in the same node, otherwise they get announced as two unrelated fragments.

INTERVIEWER: I hadn't thought about that one.

> This is the differentiator. Everyone can talk about the backend pieces. Most
> backend-leaning candidates cannot say those four things. And you brought it up
> yourself — you didn't wait to be asked.


SCENE 10 — Wrapping up
------------------------

INTERVIEWER: Anything you'd want to add?

CANDIDATE: Three things I'm aware of but didn't get into.

One is moderation — profanity and spam filtering. That hangs off the write path,
done asynchronously: show it, then pull it back if it fails.

Two is write hotspots. If one stream's comment volume saturates a single
partition, you add a random suffix to the key to spread it out and strip the
suffix before aggregating.

Three is multi-region. What I described is single-region. Across regions the
pub/sub layer has to replicate, and latency becomes the dominant problem.

INTERVIEWER: Alright, that's about time.


THREE RULES YOU CAN COPY
==========================

1. Give one layer at a time. Wait for "does that actually work" before giving
   the next one. Finish the SSE thought and stop — don't tack on Kafka and
   sharding while you're at it.

2. When you realize you said something wrong, fix it out loud. "Let me think —
   actually, no." The WebSocket to SSE switch is worth points, not against them.

3. When a calculation gives you an absurd number, park it. Don't solve it on the
   spot. Bring it back when you reach that part of the design, so the person
   listening knows what it's solving.

UBER — MOCK INTERVIEW SCRIPT
=============================

同一個候選人，技術會、流程不熟。這次犯的錯跟上一題不一樣。
對白英文，場景標題跟導演註解（>）中文。


SCENE 0 — Opening
【現在在做：釐清這題到底要設計哪一段。候選人一開口就跳技術】
----------------------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: Let's design Uber. A rider opens the app, sees a price, requests a
ride, and a driver comes and picks them up. 45 minutes.

CANDIDATE: Okay, so for finding nearby drivers we'd want a geospatial index,
probably Redis with geohashing, and then—

INTERVIEWER: You're three steps ahead. What are we even building?

CANDIDATE: Fair. Let me ask some things first.

Is this two separate apps? A rider app and a driver app?

INTERVIEWER: Yes, two apps, same backend.

CANDIDATE: Does the rider see a price before they commit, or after the ride?

INTERVIEWER: Before. They see an estimate and then decide.

CANDIDATE: And when a driver gets a request, can they decline it?

INTERVIEWER: They can.

CANDIDATE: Okay. Do I need to design payments, ratings, or the actual navigation?

INTERVIEWER: No. Assume a mapping provider gives you distance and ETA, and
payments are somebody else's problem.

> 這四問各拿到一個會改設計的答案。兩個 app 表示有兩條完全不同的資料流。先看價格表示估
> 價是獨立的一步，不是叫車的附屬。司機可以拒絕，表示媒合是一個會失敗、要重
> 試的流程，不是一次配對就結束。最後一問把導航跟金流關在門外。


SCENE 1 — Functional requirements
【現在在做：把上面問到的答案寫成三到四條規格，請面試官確認】
---------------------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: So:

  1. A rider enters pickup and destination and gets a fare estimate
  2. A rider requests a ride at that estimate
  3. The system matches them with a nearby available driver
  4. The driver accepts or declines, then navigates to pickup and drop-off

  Out of scope: payments, ratings, driver onboarding, turn-by-turn navigation

INTERVIEWER: That's the right set.

> 四條，二十秒，每一條都是使用者做得到什麼。跟上一題一樣，「不做」那行是在
> 跟面試官確認邊界。


SCENE 2 — Non-functional requirements
【現在在做：問規模。這一題的關鍵數字跟上一題完全不同，候選人差點算錯單位】
------------------------------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: How big is this?

INTERVIEWER: Global. Give me your own numbers.

CANDIDATE: Let's say 10 million active drivers worldwide. Each one has to report
its location so we can find them. How often... if a driver is moving at 60 km/h,
in 5 seconds they've moved about 80 meters. That feels like the right resolution
for matching. So every 5 seconds.

10 million drivers ÷ 5 seconds = 2 million location updates per second.

INTERVIEWER: Is that a lot?

CANDIDATE: Let me check what kind of "a lot." Each update is maybe 50 bytes of
actual data — driver id, lat, lng, timestamp. Two million of those is 100 MB a
second of raw payload. That's... honestly not much. A handful of machines.

But the write count is the problem, not the bytes. Two million writes a second
into a database, and every one of them is an UPDATE to the same row — the
driver's current position. Postgres does maybe tens of thousands of writes a
second on good hardware. We're asking for a hundred times that, and every write
generates WAL and touches an index.

INTERVIEWER: So?

CANDIDATE: So driver location doesn't go in a normal database. It goes in memory.

> 這一段是全場第一個分數點。他沒有說「兩百萬很多」就停，他問了「哪一種多」，
> 然後發現瓶頸在寫入次數不在頻寬。上一題 Facebook Live 的瓶頸是出口頻寬，這
> 題是 IOPS，兩個完全不同的東西。能講出差別，比背「用 Redis」有用得多。

CANDIDATE: Second question. When a ride request goes out to a driver, can two
different riders get matched with the same driver?

INTERVIEWER: What do you think?

CANDIDATE: Absolutely not. That's a real-world failure — two people standing on
two different corners both told the same car is coming. So matching needs strong
consistency.

Which is interesting, because most of this system doesn't. If the map shows a
driver 50 meters from where they actually are, nobody cares. Location is
eventually consistent and that's fine. But the assignment itself has to be
exactly once.

INTERVIEWER: Good.

> 這句「大部分不需要，但這一條需要」就是上一題那個模式再用一次。同一個系統
> 裡不同功能挑不同的一致性。差別在上一題的例外很小（作者看自己的留言），這
> 題的例外是整個系統的核心。

CANDIDATE: Third, latency. How long can a rider wait to get matched?

INTERVIEWER: You tell me what's acceptable.

CANDIDATE: Under a minute, and if we can't match in that time we should tell them
instead of leaving them staring at a spinner. So: match within 60 seconds or
fail visibly.

CANDIDATE: Non-functional requirements:

  - 10M drivers, ~2M location updates/sec
  - Matching within 60 seconds, or fail explicitly
  - Strong consistency on driver assignment — never double-book a driver
  - Location itself can be eventually consistent
  - Peak: 100k concurrent ride requests in one city during a surge

INTERVIEWER: Go on.


SCENE 3 — Entities and API
【現在在做：資料模型跟端點。注意 Fare 為什麼要單獨存起來】
------------------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: Entities:

  Rider     id, name, payment_method_id
  Driver    id, name, vehicle, status (offline | available | on_trip)
  Location  driver_id, lat, lng, updated_at
  Fare      id, rider_id, pickup, destination, price, eta, expires_at
  Ride      id, rider_id, driver_id, fare_id, status, timestamps

INTERVIEWER: Why is Fare its own entity? Why not just compute the price when the
ride is created?

CANDIDATE: Because the rider saw a number and agreed to it. If I recompute at
request time and traffic changed in those 30 seconds, they get charged something
different from what they tapped on. So the estimate gets persisted with an
expiry, and the ride references it.

INTERVIEWER: Good.

> 這個回答值錢，因為它不是技術理由，是產品理由。面試官問「為什麼多一張表」，
> 答「因為使用者看到的數字必須算數」比答「為了正規化」好一個等級。

CANDIDATE: API:

  POST  /fare                  { pickup, destination } -> Fare
  POST  /rides                 { fareId } -> Ride
  POST  /drivers/location      { lat, lng }
  PATCH /rides/:rideId         { action: "accept" | "decline" }

The rider app uses the first two. The driver app uses the last two.

> 最後那句一秒鐘，但它讓面試官知道你腦子裡有兩個 client，不是一個。


SCENE 4 — The dumbest version
【現在在做：先畫一個笨但完整的版本，然後自己指出它哪裡壞掉】
--------------------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: Simplest thing that satisfies all four requirements:

  Rider app  → API Gateway → Ride Service → Postgres
  Driver app → API Gateway → Ride Service → Postgres (UPDATE locations SET ...)

  Matching: SELECT * FROM locations
            WHERE lat BETWEEN ... AND lng BETWEEN ...
            AND status = 'available'

INTERVIEWER: What breaks?

CANDIDATE: Two things, and they're different kinds of broken.

The write side we already covered — two million UPDATEs a second is a hundred
times what the database can do.

The read side is worse in a subtle way. That BETWEEN query on lat and lng can't
use a normal B-tree index properly. An index on lat can narrow by latitude, but
then it still has to scan every one of those rows to filter longitude. In a
dense city that's most of your drivers, on every single ride request.

INTERVIEWER: Why can't a B-tree do it?

CANDIDATE: Because a B-tree is one-dimensional — it sorts on one axis. Two
coordinates are two axes and you can't order them into one sequence without
losing the neighbor relationship. Two points close on the map can end up far
apart in the index.

> 這是這一題的技術核心，而且他是被問到才展開的。如果第一句就說「用 geohash」，
> 面試官沒辦法判斷他懂不懂為什麼需要它。


SCENE 5 — Geospatial index
【現在在做：解讀路徑。候選人這次沒有一次倒三層，一次給一個】
--------------------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: So I need an index that keeps nearby points nearby. Geohashing does
that: it interleaves the bits of latitude and longitude into a single number, so
points that are close on the map share a long prefix.

Redis has this built in. GEOADD to write a driver's position, GEOSEARCH to ask
"give me everyone within 3 km of this point." It's in memory, so two million
writes a second is fine.

INTERVIEWER: What happens when a driver goes offline without telling you? Phone
dies, app crashes.

CANDIDATE: Their last position sits there forever and we keep matching riders to
a car that isn't there. So every location entry gets a TTL — say 30 seconds. A
driver who's actually online refreshes it every 5 seconds and never expires. One
who vanishes falls out of the index by itself.

> 這一問答是設計題的典型節奏：面試官不問「還有什麼」，問一個具體的壞掉情境，
> 看你有沒有想過。TTL 這個答案的好處是它不需要任何「偵測掉線」的程式碼。

INTERVIEWER: Two million writes a second — is all of that necessary?

CANDIDATE: No, and that's a good place to save. The client can decide how often
to report. A driver parked at home doesn't need to ping every 5 seconds — once a
minute is plenty. A driver doing 100 km/h on the highway with a pending request
nearby should ping more often.

So the interval adapts to speed, and to whether the driver is a candidate for
anything right now. That's a client-side change that cuts backend load
significantly, and it costs nothing.

> 「客戶端改一行，後端省掉一半」這種答案在面試裡很吃香，因為多數候選人只會
> 想在伺服器端加機器。


SCENE 6 — The lock
【現在在做：這題的心臟。強一致要求怎麼落到實作上】
----------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: Walk me through matching. Rider requests, what happens?

CANDIDATE: Matching service takes the request, does a GEOSEARCH for available
drivers nearby, picks the best one, and sends them the request.

INTERVIEWER: You have twenty matching service instances running. Two riders
request at the same time and both searches return the same driver.

CANDIDATE: Right, that's the double-booking case. Both instances see the driver
as available because neither has written anything yet.

So the driver has to be locked before the request goes out. Whoever acquires the
lock is the only one allowed to offer that driver.

INTERVIEWER: Where does that lock live?

CANDIDATE: Not in the application — twenty separate processes can't coordinate
through their own memory. It has to be somewhere all of them can see. Redis, with
SET driver:123 lock_value NX EX 10 — set only if it doesn't exist, expiring in
10 seconds.

INTERVIEWER: Why the expiry?

CANDIDATE: Because the instance holding the lock can die. If the lock has no
expiry and the process crashes between locking and releasing, that driver is
unavailable forever and nobody notices. The TTL means the worst case is the
driver sits out for 10 seconds.

Ten also happens to be about how long we'd give a driver to respond anyway, so
the two line up.

> 這一整段是這題最該練熟的。三個層次：為什麼要鎖（競態）、鎖放哪（跨行程所
> 以要外部）、為什麼要過期（持鎖者會死）。少講任何一層，面試官都會追。

INTERVIEWER: The driver declines. Then what?

CANDIDATE: Release the lock, go back to the candidate list, offer it to the next
driver. Same thing if they just don't respond and the 10 seconds run out.

INTERVIEWER: And if the matching service crashes in the middle of that loop?

CANDIDATE: Then the rider is waiting and nobody is working on their request. The
loop state lived in that process's memory and it's gone.

Which means the state can't live in memory. I'd use a durable workflow engine —
Temporal, or Step Functions. The workflow is "try driver 1, wait 10s, try driver
2, wait 10s..." and its state is persisted at every step. If the worker dies,
another one picks the workflow up where it stopped.

> 面試官連續三問把他推到需要 workflow engine，而不是他一開始就搬出來。這個順
> 序讓「為什麼需要它」變得明顯。如果一開始就講 Temporal，聽起來像在報菜名。


SCENE 7 — The surge
【現在在做：處理尖峰。跟上一題的 mega-stream 是同一個位置的問題】
------------------------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: A concert ends. 100,000 people request a ride in the same
neighborhood within two minutes.

CANDIDATE: Right now those requests go straight into the matching service, so
the matching service has to absorb the entire spike. If it can't keep up,
requests time out and riders get nothing — and the ones that were in flight when
an instance died are lost entirely.

So requests go into a queue instead. Kafka. The matching service consumes at
whatever rate it can manage, and the queue absorbs the difference. Offsets are
committed only after a match succeeds, so a crash means the request gets picked
up again rather than disappearing.

INTERVIEWER: Doesn't that make riders wait longer?

CANDIDATE: It makes some riders wait longer, yes. But the alternative is that
everyone gets a degraded service and some requests silently vanish. A queue turns
"random failure" into "predictable wait," and I can show the rider a real
position — "we're finding you a driver" with a number that means something.

I'd also partition the queue geographically. A concert in one city shouldn't
delay matching in another.

> 「把隨機失敗換成可預測的等待」這句話可以整句背起來。佇列的價值講不清楚的候
> 選人很多，多數只會說「削峰」。

INTERVIEWER: And scaling globally?

CANDIDATE: Geo-shard the whole stack. A ride in Taipei never needs data from
Berlin — the working set is naturally local. So each region gets its own Redis,
its own queue, its own matching service, and requests are routed by region at
the edge.

The awkward part is city boundaries. A rider standing at the edge of two shards
needs candidates from both, so those requests fan out to two regions and merge.
That's a small fraction of traffic and it's worth the complexity everywhere else.


SCENE 8 — Frontend lens
【現在在做：主動把前端那一半講出來，不等面試官問】
----------------------------------------------------

CANDIDATE: Can I spend a few minutes on the client side? There are decisions
there that the backend design assumes but doesn't enforce.

INTERVIEWER: Please.

CANDIDATE: First, the connection type. For live driver position during a ride I'd
use WebSocket rather than SSE — the opposite of what I'd pick for a comment feed.
The rider sends things back mid-ride: cancel, change destination, message the
driver. That reverse channel is real here, so the bidirectional cost is earned.

Second, state. The fare estimate is server state with an expiry, and the UI has
to respect that expiry — showing a stale price with a "request" button under it
is how you end up charging someone a number they never agreed to. When it
expires, the button re-fetches instead of submitting.

Third, and this is where I'd push back on a product request. "Finding a driver"
can show optimistically the moment the rider taps. "Driver found, Toyota Prius,
4 minutes away" cannot. That comes from the lock on the server, and if the client
guesses it, we've shown a rider a car that was never assigned to them. The rule
I'd write down: optimistic updates are fine for state the client owns, never for
state a server lock produces.

Fourth, the map is pure visual. A blind rider needs the driver's distance, ETA,
and vehicle description as text that a screen reader reaches without hunting.
And the confirm-pickup control needs a large touch target, because half the
people using the driver app are looking at it with a car in gear.

INTERVIEWER: The optimistic distinction is a good rule.

> 第三點是這一整份腳本裡最能拉開差距的一段。它同時展示了三件事：知道
> optimistic update 是什麼、知道它什麼時候不能用、而且理由是從後端的鎖推出來
> 的。這是全端的證據，不是宣稱。


SCENE 9 — Wrapping up
【現在在做：主動列出沒講到的，並說明為什麼是那三個】
------------------------------------------------------

INTERVIEWER: What did we not cover?

CANDIDATE: Three things I'd want to come back to.

Surge pricing — the fare estimate would need to read live supply and demand for
the area, which makes pricing a real-time system rather than a lookup.

Driver incentives in matching — I picked "nearest driver," but a real system
balances acceptance rate, driver earnings, and time-to-pickup. That's a policy
question sitting on top of the same infrastructure.

And the second half of the ride. Everything I designed stops at "driver
accepted." Tracking the trip, handling the driver going through a tunnel and
losing signal for four minutes, detecting that the ride actually ended — that's
a whole second system.

INTERVIEWER: Good. That's time.

的 requirements 不能跳過。


三條規則（跟上一題同一份，這次多一條）
========================================

1. 一次只給一層，等他問「這樣夠嗎」再給下一層。
2. 講錯就當場改口，「想一下，其實不對」。
3. 算出荒謬的數字先記著，走到那一步再拿出來解。
4. 被問到「為什麼要這個元件」的時候，答案應該是前一個問題，不是它的功能。
   Temporal 的理由是「服務掛了迴圈狀態會消失」，不是「它是 workflow engine」。

# H3 (Hexagonal Hierarchical Geospatial Index)

> [Geohash](chunk://geohash) and [quadtree](chunk://quadtree) both use square cells. A square has 8 neighbors, but the 4 that share an edge are closer and the 4 that share a corner are farther, so "neighbor" means two different distances. When you spread outward by one ring, a cell in the corner direction is about 1.4 times as far from the center as one in the edge direction, so the spread comes out square shaped. H3 uses hexagons instead.

## What hexagons do better

A hexagon has only 6 neighbors, and **the distance from each neighbor's center to its own center is almost the same**. A square cannot do this.

```text
square: 8 neighbors                             hexagon: 6 neighbors
  ┌────┬────┬────┐                                ⬡ ⬡
  │near│near│near│  ← corners far, edges near    ⬡ ⬢ ⬡   ← all six about the same distance
  ├────┼────┼────┤                                ⬡ ⬡
  │near│ me │near│
  ├────┼────┼────┤
  │near│near│near│
  └────┴────┴────┘
```

For calculations that "spread out ring by ring from one point", within each ring every hexagon is about the same distance from the center, so the spread comes out close to a circle. Uber uses it for supply and demand hot zones, dynamic pricing, and driver distribution, all problems of this spreading kind.

## Hierarchy

H3 was developed by Uber. It tiles the whole Earth with a hexagonal grid at 16 resolutions. Each step down, one hexagon is split into roughly 7 smaller hexagons. Each cell has a 64-bit id, and the resolution is encoded in the id itself.

Hexagons cannot be subdivided recursively as cleanly as squares. The seven child cells only approximate the parent's boundary; they do not line up exactly. That is the price of uniform neighbor distance.

## Three grids compared

| | geohash | quadtree | H3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell shape | square | square | hexagon |
| Cell size | fixed | adaptive | fixed (hierarchical) |
| Neighbor distance | uneven | uneven | almost uniform |
| Best at | storing in a DB, prefix queries | uneven point distribution | spreading, movement, neighborhood analysis |
| Typical users | Redis GEO, ES | games, images | Uber, geo analysis |

## When to pick H3

Ride-hailing supply and demand matching, cutting a city into uniform cells for statistics, tracking movement flows: all of these look at how one cell relates to the cells around it. H3's neighbor distance is uniform, so it fits these calculations better than squares. A plain "find restaurants within 5 km" only asks what is nearby; [geohash](chunk://geohash) or PostGIS is enough, and H3 is not needed.
